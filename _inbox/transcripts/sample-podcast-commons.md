# The Commons Podcast - Episode 42: Digital Tools for Community Governance

## [00:00:30] Introduction

**Sarah Martinez**: Welcome back to The Commons Podcast, where we explore how communities self-organize and govern shared resources. I'm your host Sarah Martinez, and today we're joined by Dr. Elinor Blake, who's been researching digital tools for community governance at the Digital Democracy Lab. Welcome, Elinor.

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: Thanks for having me, Sarah. I'm excited to share what we've been learning.

## [00:01:45] The Digital Governance Landscape

**Sarah Martinez**: Let's start with the big picture. What does the landscape of digital governance tools look like right now?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: It's actually quite diverse. We've categorized these tools into several groups. First, you have voting and polling tools - things like Pol.is, which uses machine learning to find consensus points in large-scale discussions. Then there are deliberation platforms like Decidim, which originated in Barcelona and is now used by cities worldwide.

You also have coordination tools that aren't specifically for governance but get repurposed - Loomio for decision-making, Slack or Discord for communication. And increasingly, we're seeing tools specifically designed around particular governance models, like tools for sociocratic organizations.

**Sarah Martinez**: What's driving this growth in digital governance tools?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: A few things. The pandemic accelerated remote collaboration, which made digital governance tools essential for many groups. Climate organizing has brought young, tech-savvy people into civic spaces. And frankly, traditional institutions have lost trust, so people are experimenting with alternatives.

## [00:08:20] Participatory Budgeting Technology

**Sarah Martinez**: You mentioned participatory budgeting earlier. Can you talk about how technology is changing that?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: Participatory budgeting, or PB, has been around since Porto Alegre in 1989, but it was mostly in-person. Now we're seeing digital platforms that can dramatically expand participation.

Stanford's Participatory Budgeting Project developed a method called "knapsack voting" where participants allocate a virtual budget across different proposals. This scales much better than traditional assembly-based PB.

Cities like Paris have put hundreds of millions of euros through participatory budgeting using digital platforms. New York City, Boston, and many others have their own programs.

**Sarah Martinez**: What are the challenges with digital PB?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: The digital divide is a major one. Not everyone has equal access to technology. And there's a risk that digital participation becomes shallow - clicking a button is different from deliberating with neighbors. The best implementations combine digital and in-person elements.

## [00:15:30] Deliberative Democracy Tools

**Sarah Martinez**: Let's talk about deliberative democracy. There's been a lot of interest in citizen assemblies lately.

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: Yes, citizens' assemblies are having a moment. Ireland used them for constitutional reform. France had the Citizens' Convention on Climate. And there are hundreds happening at local levels.

The interesting thing for digital tools is the preparation and follow-up phases. Before an assembly, you need to inform randomly selected participants about complex issues. Digital learning modules, expert videos, and curated information packages are all being developed.

During assemblies, tools like Mentimeter or real-time polling help facilitators gauge the room. And after, there's the challenge of communicating decisions back to the broader public and tracking implementation.

## [00:22:45] Blockchain and DAOs

**Sarah Martinez**: I have to ask about blockchain. There's been a lot of hype about DAOs - Decentralized Autonomous Organizations. Is that relevant to community governance?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: It's a mixed picture. The core idea of DAOs - encoding governance rules in smart contracts, using tokens for voting - has some interesting properties. It's transparent, hard to tamper with, and can enable global coordination.

But there are serious limitations. Token-based voting often recreates plutocracy - whoever owns the most tokens has the most power. And the "code is law" philosophy can be rigid. Communities need to adapt and interpret their rules, which requires human judgment.

That said, some experiments are interesting. Gitcoin's quadratic funding mechanism helps fund public goods. And some DAOs are exploring more nuanced governance models.

**Sarah Martinez**: What's quadratic funding?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: It's a matching mechanism where small donations from many people are matched more than large donations from few people. So if a hundred people each give one dollar to a project, that might get more matching funds than one person giving a hundred dollars. It helps surface broadly supported public goods.

## [00:32:10] AI and Governance

**Sarah Martinez**: What about artificial intelligence? Is AI being used in governance tools?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: Increasingly, yes. Pol.is uses machine learning to cluster opinions and find consensus. There's research on using language models to summarize public comments, which could help decision-makers understand thousands of inputs.

But there are important concerns. AI can encode biases. It can be used to manipulate rather than inform. And there's a risk of what I call "governance theater" - appearing to consult the public while actually using AI to manufacture desired outcomes.

We need governance AI that's transparent, accountable, and actually empowers communities rather than centralizing control.

## [00:38:45] Local Implementation

**Sarah Martinez**: For our listeners who might want to bring some of these tools to their communities, what advice would you give?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: Start with your goals, not the technology. What problem are you trying to solve? Who needs to be involved? What kind of decisions are you making?

Then look at your context. A rural community with limited internet is different from an urban neighborhood with lots of tech workers. Accessibility matters enormously.

I'd also suggest starting small and iterating. Pilot a tool with a subset of decisions or a smaller group. Learn what works. Then scale.

And importantly, remember that tools are just tools. The relationships, trust, and culture of your community matter more than any technology.

## [00:45:20] The Future of Digital Governance

**Sarah Martinez**: What are you most excited about for the future?

**Dr. Elinor Blake**: I'm excited about the convergence of several trends. Deliberative methods are getting better tested. Digital tools are getting more mature. And there's growing demand for new forms of participation.

I think we'll see more hybrid models - combining online and offline, combining expert input with citizen deliberation, combining different decision-making methods for different contexts.

I'm also hopeful about the democratization of these tools. It used to take significant resources to run a participatory process. Now, smaller communities can access sophisticated tools.
