# Consent-Based Decision Making

## Summary

A decision-making pattern where proposals are adopted unless there are meaningful, reasoned objections based on harm to the group's aims. Unlike consensus (where everyone must agree it's the best option), consent asks: "Is this good enough for now, safe enough to try?"

## Context

Use this pattern when:
- A group needs to make decisions efficiently while ensuring all voices are heard
- Traditional voting creates win/lose dynamics that harm group cohesion
- Consensus processes are too slow or result in watered-down compromises
- You want to encourage experimentation with reversible decisions

## Problem

Groups often struggle with decision-making that is both efficient and inclusive. Majority voting can marginalize minorities. Consensus can be slow and lead to lowest-common-denominator outcomes. Both can create frustration and disengagement.

## Solution

Implement consent-based decision making with these elements:

### The Consent Threshold

A proposal passes if no member has a "reasoned objection" - an objection based on how the proposal would harm the group's ability to achieve its aims. Personal preferences alone don't block proposals.

### The Process

1. **Proposal**: A clear proposal is presented in writing
2. **Clarifying Questions**: Members ask questions to understand (not to debate)
3. **Reactions**: Quick round of initial thoughts and feelings
4. **Amend**: Proposer may amend based on feedback
5. **Consent Round**: Each member indicates consent or raises objection
6. **Integration**: If objections, work to integrate concerns

### Handling Objections

Valid objections:
- "This would harm our ability to achieve [stated aim]"
- "This creates a safety concern for [specific reason]"
- "This contradicts our agreed policy on [topic]"

Not valid objections:
- "I would do it differently"
- "I don't like this approach"
- "I'm not sure it's the best option"

## Consequences

**Benefits:**
- Faster decisions than consensus
- Clear criteria for what blocks a proposal
- Encourages experimentation ("safe enough to try")
- Everyone speaks in rounds, surfacing quiet voices
- Builds decision-making capacity over time

**Liabilities:**
- Requires skilled facilitation
- Takes practice to distinguish valid objections
- Can feel uncomfortable for those used to voting
- Psychological safety is prerequisite

## Examples

### Neighborhood Council

A neighborhood council adopted consent for policy decisions. Meeting time for decisions dropped by 40%, while member satisfaction with decisions increased. They maintained voting for officer elections where consent wasn't appropriate.

### Worker Cooperative

A tech cooperative uses consent for all business decisions under $10,000. Larger decisions require consent at a general meeting. This enables nimble responses while preserving collective ownership of major choices.

## Related Patterns

- **Advice Process**: For operational decisions, combine with consent for policy
- **Sociocracy**: A complete governance system built on consent
- **Round Robin**: The structured speaking order used in consent processes

## Implementation Notes

Start with low-stakes decisions to build skill. Common mistakes include:
- Moving too fast through the consent round
- Not giving enough time for clarifying questions
- Facilitator accepting objections without exploring if they're reasoned
